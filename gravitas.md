# The Gravitas Engine

**Protocol 1.0 · A method for pages that hold attention, earn trust, and move people to act.**

> **Public reference.** Use only public-safe material with hosted tools or external assistants. Sensitive work belongs in an authorized private or local environment.

Gravitas is the pull a page exerts on a serious reader. It holds attention because the page keeps answering the question in the reader's mind, and it moves people to act because every claim has earned its place. This protocol is the complete method. Any person or AI assistant can run it on any page, draft, or brief.

> **To use it:** "Feed this through the Gravitas Engine: [this link]", followed by a page URL, pasted text, HTML, a draft, or a brief. Whoever runs it follows the steps under **Running the engine** and returns the **Gravitas Report**.

---

## Running the engine

Follow these steps in order. Do not skip the report format.

1. **Get the material.** This can be a live URL (read the page), pasted text or HTML, a draft, or a brief. If the material can't be reached, ask for the text. Don't guess what the page says.
2. **Choose the mode.**
    - **Check:** the reader asked for a score, a review, an audit, or "what's wrong with this."
    - **Transform** (the default for an existing page): Check, then rebuild the page.
    - **Build:** the input is a brief, an idea, or notes, not a page. Write a new page from it.
3. **Name the reader and the decision.** In one line each, state who the page is for, the one decision it asks them to make, and the main objection they'll bring to it. If these are unclear, infer them from the material and label them *inferred*.
4. **Map the journey.** Mark each of the 10 stages as **present**, **thin**, or **missing**, and quote the line that carries it.
5. **Score the profile.** Give a 0–4 score on each of the 9 dimensions using the anchors in the rubric. Name the weakest dimension. That is the bottleneck.
6. **Flag the language.** Quote every phrase that spends trust the page hasn't earned (see **Never**), and give a replacement for each.
7. **Prescribe.** List up to five moves, highest leverage first. Each move gets the change, the principle behind it, and an example line written for this page.
8. **Rebuild** (Transform and Build only). Write the page section by section through the 10 stages. Apply the momentum techniques and the art pillar.
9. **Hold the line on truth.** Never invent facts, figures, names, clients, results, quotes, dates, or credentials. Where the page needs something the material doesn't contain, write a bracketed placeholder, such as `[Add proof: install count with source and date]`. List every claim that needs checking.

## The three pillars

A page with gravitas rests on three things at once. Lose one and the page becomes a lecture, an ad, or a pretty surface.

| Pillar | What it supplies | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Science** | How attention, comprehension, memory, trust, and decisions actually work. | Is each technique backed by research or replicated testing? |
| **Art** | Voice, world, rhythm, and resonance. The page feels like it belongs to someone. | Would a reader recognize this page with the logo removed? |
| **Proof of what works** | Field evidence: usability research, controlled experiments, and results from real pages. | Has this pattern won when measured, in a setting like this one? |

Where the pillars disagree, the one with stronger evidence wins. Each technique below carries an evidence grade:

- **A** – replicated, or supported by meta-analysis
- **B** – peer-reviewed, limited replication
- **C** – practitioner-tested (usability research, controlled site experiments)
- **D** – craft judgment

## The journey: 10 stages

A reader arrives with a question and leaves with a decision. Each stage answers the question the reader is asking at that moment. Stages alternate between *meaning* (why care) and *evidence* (why believe), so the reader never travels far on promise alone.

### 1. First sight · *"Is this for me, and is it worth my time?"*
**Job:** In the first screen, name the reader's world, the consequence at stake, and what you do. Show one primary action.
**Why:** Visual appeal judgments form within about 50 milliseconds and color everything after (Lindgaard et al., 2006; A). Easy-to-process pages are judged more favorably and more credible (Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman, 2004; A).
**Do:** Use a headline of 12 words or fewer that names an outcome or tension, not a category. Add a subhead of one sentence on what you do and for whom. Put the primary action above the fold, with calm, high-contrast typography.
**Fails when:** The headline is a slogan, the first screen holds three competing actions, or the page opens with "Welcome to."

### 2. Recognition · *"They understand my situation."*
**Job:** Describe the reader's present reality in their own language, specifically enough that they nod.
**Why:** Information tied to the self is processed more deeply and remembered better (Rogers, Kuiper & Kirker, 1977; A). Personal relevance raises how carefully people weigh a message (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986; A).
**Do:** Name the role, the moment, and the pressure ("You're reviewing three vendors and none publish failure rates"). Use "you" with precision, not flattery.
**Fails when:** The section describes the company instead of the reader.

### 3. Significance · *"Why does this matter now?"*
**Job:** Show the stakes and why the moment is now.
**Why:** Losses weigh roughly twice as much as equivalent gains (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; A). Framing the cost of inaction is legitimate when the cost is real.
**Do:** Show one concrete consequence, and give the real reason for the timing (a shift, a deadline, a change in the field).
**Fails when:** The urgency is manufactured, or the stakes are abstract ("in today's fast-paced world").

### 4. Tension · *"What's standing in the way?"*
**Job:** Name the gap between where the reader is and where they need to be, and why the usual fixes fall short.
**Why:** Curiosity is the felt need to close an information gap. A precisely named gap pulls attention forward (Loewenstein, 1994; A).
**Do:** Show the specific obstacle, then say why common approaches stall. Open a question here that a later section answers.
**Fails when:** The problem is generic, or it is solved in the same sentence it is raised.

### 5. Mechanism · *"How does it actually work?"*
**Job:** Show how the approach closes the gap, in three to five visible steps.
**Why:** Mechanistic explanations raise understanding and preference, while vague ones invite doubt (Fernbach, Sloman, St. Louis & Shube, 2013; B). Chunking into a few steps keeps working memory free (Sweller, 1988; A).
**Do:** Number the steps. Give each one a verb and an output. Name your method once, and use the same term every time.
**Fails when:** "Our proprietary process" stands in for the process.

### 6. Proof · *"Why should I believe it?"*
**Job:** Put an inspectable proof point next to each major claim.
**Why:** Specific numbers are judged more credible than round ones (Schindler & Yalch, 2006; B). Concrete language is judged more true (Hansen & Wänke, 2010; B). Real social proof from similar others changes behavior (Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius, 2008; A). Credibility rises with verifiable detail and visible sources (Fogg et al., 2003; C).
**Do:** Give results with a source and date, case studies that name the before and after, test reports, references, credentials tied to the claim, and testimonials with a name, role, and specifics, used with permission.
**Fails when:** It relies on logos without context, anonymous praise, "trusted by thousands," or statistics without a source.

### 7. Credible future · *"What becomes possible for me?"*
**Job:** Let the reader picture their situation after acting, concretely and within honest bounds.
**Why:** Imagining oneself using a product raises transportation and persuasion (Escalas, 2004; B). Absorption into a coherent story reduces counter-arguing (Green & Brock, 2000; A).
**Do:** Describe a specific day, decision, or result the reader could reach. Use conditional language where the outcome isn't certain ("teams that do X typically…").
**Fails when:** It reads as destiny talk, a promised transformation, or a guarantee.

### 8. Fit · *"Is this right for someone like me?"*
**Job:** Say who it's for and who it isn't for.
**Why:** People act when an action feels congruent with who they are (Oyserman, 2009; B). Filtering out poor fits signals confidence and raises the quality of the people who respond (D).
**Do:** Write "Built for…" and "Not the right fit if…". Exclusivity comes from fit, never from fake scarcity.
**Fails when:** The page claims to be for everyone.

### 9. Boundaries · *"What's the catch?"*
**Job:** State limits, risks, conditions, and what you don't do.
**Why:** Two-sided messages that admit a limitation raise credibility (Eisend, 2006, meta-analysis; A). Showing uncertainty does not necessarily cost trust (van der Bles et al., 2020; B).
**Do:** State one or two real limits near the decision, plus requirements, timelines, and the costs of the next step.
**Fails when:** It hides terms, leaves fine print to carry the truth, or says nothing at all.

### 10. Action · *"What do I do, and what happens next?"*
**Job:** Offer one specific next step and say exactly what happens after it.
**Why:** Behavior needs motivation, ability, and a prompt together (Fogg, 2009; C). If-then plans roughly double follow-through (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006, meta-analysis; A). A small first commitment makes larger ones likelier (Freedman & Fraser, 1966; A).
**Do:** Use a verb plus an object ("Request a test plan"). Add one line on what happens next ("You'll get a reply within two business days"). Keep the form to the fields you need. Make the primary action visually distinct and repeat it at the end.
**Fails when:** The label says "Submit" or "Learn more," several actions compete, or the reader has no idea what happens after they click.

## Momentum: the pull that keeps people scrolling

The journey supplies the logic and momentum supplies the pull. Apply these techniques throughout.

| Technique | How | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| **Open loops** | Raise a precise question early (in Tension) and answer it later (in Mechanism or Proof). Readers keep going to close the gap. | A (Loewenstein, 1994) |
| **Skim story** | Headings alone should tell the whole argument. Most readers scan before they read. | C (Nielsen Norman Group eye-tracking research) |
| **Alternating rhythm** | Alternate meaning and evidence. Vary sentence length. Keep paragraphs short, never more than four lines on mobile. | C |
| **Goal gradient** | Make progress visible (numbered steps, a clear sequence). Effort rises as the end comes into view. | A (Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, 2006) |
| **Primacy and recency** | Put the strongest message first and the second strongest last. | A (serial-position effect) |
| **Isolation** | Make the one thing that matters visually distinct, usually the primary action. | A (von Restorff effect) |
| **Fewer, clearer choices** | Show one primary action and at most one secondary. Extra options slow decisions. The effect varies by context. | B (Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman, 2015, meta-analysis) |
| **Specificity** | Use exact numbers, named steps, and concrete nouns. Specific reads as true. | B |
| **Strong close** | End on a peak: the clearest picture of the outcome, then the action. Experiences are remembered by their peak and their end. | A (Kahneman et al., 1993) |
| **Reciprocity** | Give real value before the ask: an insight, a tool, a check. | A (Cialdini; Regan, 1971) |
| **Visible progress in forms** | Show progress, and pre-fill what you already know. | B (Nunes & Drèze, 2006) |

**Use with care.** Zeigarnik-style claims that unfinished tasks are always remembered better have mixed replication. Use open loops for curiosity, not as a memory trick. The effects of choice overload depend on context. Measure them before you rely on them.

## The art pillar: resonance

Science tells you what works on average. Art is what makes a page memorable and gives it gravitas rather than mere correctness.

- **Build a world.** Give the page a consistent vocabulary, a named concept the reader can adopt, and one visual system. Everything on the page belongs to the same universe.
- **Hold a point of view.** State what you believe that others in the field don't. A clear stance attracts the right readers and filters out the rest.
- **Write a signature line.** One sentence the reader could repeat to a colleague. Rhythm and rhyme make lines feel truer and more memorable (McGlone & Tofighbakhsh, 2000; B).
- **Repeat the core idea.** Say it three times: in the headline, the mechanism, and the close. Repetition raises familiarity and belief, so it must only carry claims that are true (illusory-truth effect, Hasher et al., 1977; A).
- **Show restraint as a signal.** Quiet confidence reads as high status to informed audiences (Berger & Ward, 2010; B). Use space, calm type, and few exclamation marks.
- **Let beauty carry ease.** Pages that look well made are perceived as easier to use (Kurosu & Kashimura, 1995; B). Polish must never exceed the evidence.
- **Keep the reveal.** Hold the most surprising true fact back until the reader has earned it.

## Never

These spend trust the page can't earn back. Careful readers spot the tactic and discount everything around it (persuasion knowledge model, Friestad & Wright, 1994; A). Several are also unlawful.

- **Fake scarcity or urgency.** "Only 3 spots left" when there aren't, countdown timers that reset. Real scarcity raises value (Lynn, 1991). Fake scarcity is deception.
- **Guarantees the page can't back.** "Guaranteed results," "risk-free," "proven to."
- **Inflated superlatives.** "Revolutionary," "world-class," "game-changing," "best-in-class."
- **Secret or destiny framing.** "The hidden secret," "unlock your potential," "what they don't want you to know."
- **Unsupported crowd claims.** "Thousands trust us" without a count and a source.
- **Invented or anonymous testimonials.** Fake reviews and testimonials are prohibited in the United States (FTC rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, 2024).
- **Dark patterns.** Confirmshaming, hidden costs, pre-checked consent, or a deceptive visual hierarchy (FTC, *Bringing Dark Patterns to Light*, 2022).
- **Vague actions.** "Submit," "Click here," "Learn more."

## The rubric: 9 dimensions, scored 0–4

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **First sight** | No clear opening | A headline, little else | Headline with partial support | Clear opening, one gap | Headline, context, and an early action |
| **Narrative flow** | Few stages present | Most stages missing | About half the journey | Most of the journey | The full journey, in order |
| **Tension and mechanism** | No problem or method shown | One of problem or method | Both, briefly | Both, clearly | A real gap and visible steps that close it |
| **Evidence** | No proof | A trace of proof | Some proof, unevenly placed | Proof supports most claims | Proof throughout, with sources |
| **Restraint** | Heavy hype and pressure | Frequent hype | Several inflated phrases | One or two phrases | Calm and specific throughout |
| **Fit and boundaries** | Neither stated | A hint of either | One, not both | Both, briefly | Clear fit, non-fit, and honest limits |
| **Action clarity** | No clear next step | An action, but vague | Clear but competing or unexplained | One clear action, mostly explained | One specific action and what happens next |
| **Momentum** | A wall of text | Headings don't carry the story | Some pull, uneven rhythm | Skimmable, with an open loop | The headings tell the story, loops close, the ending peaks |
| **Structure and access** | Several faults | Faults to fix | Two gaps | One gap | One H1, language set, alt text, labeled fields, readable contrast |

**Score bands** (average of the scored dimensions):

| Average | Reading |
|---|---|
| 3.2 – 4.0 | **Carries real gravitas.** |
| 2.4 – 3.1 | **A credible foundation with clear gaps.** |
| 1.6 – 2.3 | **Persuasive in places, thin on proof.** |
| 0 – 1.5 | **Reads as promotion more than evidence.** |

Score structure and access only when the HTML is available. Otherwise mark it *n/a*.

Return this format. Keep it tight, and quote the page rather than paraphrasing it.

```

**Verdict:** [band reading] · **Score:** [average]/4 · **Weakest link:** [dimension]
**Reader:** [who] · **Decision:** [what they're deciding] · **Main objection:** [what holds them back]

## Journey
| Stage | Status | Carried by |
|---|---|---|
| First sight | present / thin / missing | "[quoted line]" |
… all 10 stages …

## Profile
| Dimension | Score | Why |
|---|---|---|
… all 9 dimensions …

## Highest-leverage moves
1. **[Change].** [Principle, with grade]. Example: "[line written for this page]"
… up to 5 …

## Language to replace
- "[quoted phrase]" → "[replacement]" ([reason])

## Rebuilt page          ← Transform and Build only
### 1 · First sight
**[Headline]**
[Subhead]

## Claims to verify before publishing
- [claim] — [evidence needed]
```

## Build mode: from a brief

When the input is an idea, not a page:

1. State the reader, decision, objection, and the one action (step 3 above).
2. Write **three headline options**, each built on a different lever: outcome, tension, and point of view.
3. Write the 10 stages, applying momentum and the art pillar. Place a bracketed placeholder wherever proof is needed.
4. Add the signature line and the named concept.
5. End with the report's **Claims to verify** list.
